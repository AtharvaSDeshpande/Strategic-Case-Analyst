# Internal assessment

## When to use it

Use this when two firms sit in the same industry and perform differently, or when a firm claims an
advantage and you need to test the claim. Industry analysis explains the arena; this explains why
one player does better in it.

It is also the right tool when a firm is asset-rich and result-poor. The framework has a specific
diagnosis for that situation, and it is not "the assets are bad."

## The framework steps

The resource-based view rests on two assumptions worth stating, because the analysis is invalid
where they fail: firms in the same industry hold **different** bundles of resources, and those
bundles are **hard to move or copy**. Where every player can buy the same inputs on an open market,
resource-based reasoning has nothing to work with.

Within that, VRIO is a **sequential filter**, not a four-column scorecard. Each test is a gate. A
resource that fails a gate does not proceed, and testing a later gate on a resource that already
failed an earlier one is an analytical error, not merely wasted effort.

1. **Valuable.** Does it let the firm exploit an opportunity or neutralise a threat?
   *No* means competitive disadvantage. The question becomes whether to fix, outsource or exit it.
   *Yes* moves to the next gate.
2. **Rare.** Is it held by only a few competitors?
   *No* means competitive **parity**. Stop here. Parity is not a weakness: it is usually the price
   of being allowed to compete at all. But it cannot explain outperformance, and scoring it as a
   strength is the most common way these analyses go wrong.
   *Yes* moves on.
3. **Inimitable.** Is it costly or slow for a rival to copy or substitute? The usual reasons are
   historical conditions that cannot be replayed, causal ambiguity about what actually produces the
   result, and social complexity that resists being bought.
   *No* means **temporary** advantage. Say how long you think the window is and what closes it.
   *Yes* moves on.
4. **Organised.** Is the firm structured, resourced and incentivised to capture the value?
   *No* means **unrealised** advantage: the firm holds something genuinely rare and hard to copy and
   gets nothing for it.
   *Yes* means **sustained** competitive advantage, which is what "core competence" properly means.

## How to apply it well

- **Respect the sequence.** Do not test inimitability on something that failed rarity. The output
  of VRIO is a verdict per resource, and the verdict is determined by the first gate that fails.
- **The "organised" test is about fit with the current environment, not about being well run.** An
  organisation built precisely for yesterday's conditions is well organised for a world that no
  longer exists. When the environment shifts, an arrangement that was the source of advantage can
  become the thing preventing capture. Always ask "organised for *what* environment," and check
  whether that environment still holds.
- **Look for unrealised advantage deliberately.** It is the most under-diagnosed verdict and the
  most actionable, because the fix is internal. A useful signal: the firm can describe the asset in
  detail but cannot point to a revenue, retention or cost number attributable to it.
- **Demand an output, not an existence proof, before marking O as met.** A resource exists is not
  the same as a resource pays. If no disclosed number moves because of it, the honest mark is
  "untested," and untested should be visible in the table rather than quietly rounded up.
- **Convert scale claims into rarity tests.** Being large is valuable and very often not rare,
  because the nearest rival is large too. Check the rival's position before treating size as an
  advantage.
- **Watch for the mirror error.** The resources most likely to be genuinely rare and inimitable,
  such as an unusual ownership structure or a hard-won relationship, are frequently the ones with
  no evidenced output. Strength on V, R and I plus silence on O is a finding, not an oversight to
  be smoothed over.
- **Separate the resource from the result.** Good margins are a result. The question is which
  resource produces them and whether that resource passes the gates.
- **Pair it with the external view.** An attractive position the firm cannot defend, and a strong
  capability aimed at an unattractive position, fail for opposite reasons. Neither framework catches
  both.

## Common pitfalls

- Marking a valuable resource as an advantage without testing rarity, so parity is reported as
  strength.
- Filling all four columns for every resource, which hides which gate actually decided the verdict.
- Treating "organised" as a judgment about management quality rather than about alignment with the
  current environment.
- Listing resources that no competitor lacks, such as basic infrastructure, and calling the list an
  assessment.
- Inferring the organised condition from good overall results, which assumes the conclusion.
- Presenting the framework's labels as the analysis. "Valuable, rare, inimitable" is a verdict; the
  analysis is the evidence and the mechanism behind it.

## Quality checks

- Does every resource carry a single verdict, and is the deciding gate identifiable?
- Is any resource marked "sustained" without a named output it produces?
- Are parity verdicts present? An assessment where everything is an advantage has not been tested.
- For each "organised" mark, is the environment it is organised *for* stated?
- Are resources described specifically enough that a reader could check them, rather than named in
  the abstract?
- Is at least one uncomfortable verdict recorded? Analyses that flatter throughout are rarely
  complete.

## Standard diagram convention

The industry-standard form here is a **table**, not a picture, and that is a deliberate choice
rather than a shortcut. Rows are resources and capabilities. Columns are Valuable, Rare,
Inimitable, Organised, and a final Competitive implication column carrying the verdict.

Mark cells consistently with Yes, No or Partial, and leave cells after the deciding gate visibly
blank or marked "not tested" rather than filled in. The blanks are informative: they show the
reader where each resource stopped and why.

If you want a visual companion, plot the resources on a simple two-axis chart of durability against
evidenced value capture, which makes the unrealised-advantage quadrant visible at a glance. Keep it
secondary to the table; the table is the deliverable.
