# Industry analysis

## When to use it

Reach for this when the question is about the arena rather than the player: is this industry
structurally attractive, who captures the profit in it, and what is changing underneath it. It is
the natural first pass before any firm-level assessment, because a firm's results are partly a
reading of the industry it sits in.

It is the wrong tool when the question is why two firms in the same industry perform differently.
Structure is common to everyone in the industry, so it cannot by itself explain a gap between
rivals. Move to internal assessment for that.

## The framework steps

**Define the industry boundary first.** Everything downstream depends on it. Write down which
products, which buyer groups and which geography you are treating as inside the industry, and what
you are deliberately leaving outside. A boundary drawn too wide turns real rivals into substitutes;
drawn too narrow, it hides the competitor that is actually taking your demand.

**Scan the macro environment (PESTEL).** Six categories of force that sit outside any firm's
control: political, economic, social, technological, environmental and legal. Treat each as a
question about direction and pace, not a list of nouns. The useful output is not "regulation
exists" but "this specific rule is changing in this direction on this timetable, and here is the
force it moves."

**Assess the five competitive forces.** Rivalry among existing competitors sits in the middle; the
other four press on it.

1. *Rivalry* is high when competitors are numerous or evenly matched, growth is slow so gains must
   be taken from someone, products are hard to tell apart, and exit barriers keep weak players in.
2. *Threat of new entrants* falls as entry barriers rise: capital intensity, minimum efficient
   scale, regulated licences, brand, distribution access, accumulated data.
3. *Bargaining power of suppliers* rises when supply is concentrated, the input is specialised, or
   switching supplier is costly.
4. *Bargaining power of buyers* rises when buyers are concentrated or well informed, switching
   costs are low, and the purchase is a large share of their spend.
5. *Threat of substitutes* comes from outside the industry boundary you drew, meeting the same
   underlying need by another route. It caps what the industry can charge.

**Trace structure into conduct and performance.** Structure shapes how firms behave, which shows up
in results. The chain runs structure to conduct to performance, and it is worth walking explicitly
because it forces you to say *how* a structural feature reaches the income statement.

**Rate each force and say why.** A rating with no mechanism behind it is decoration.

## How to apply it well

- **Read the forces as moving, not fixed.** The interesting question is which force is changing and
  in which direction, not what each is today. A static snapshot dates immediately and tells you
  nothing about where to stand in three years.
- **Let the macro scan drive the force ratings.** PESTEL earns its place when a macro factor is
  shown changing a specific force. A technology shift that removes a distribution barrier is an
  entry-threat finding, not a general observation about technology.
- **Segment before you generalise.** Macro forces rarely land evenly. The same shift can gut one
  segment of an industry and barely touch another. If the industry has distinct segments, rate the
  forces per segment or say plainly that the average describes nobody.
- **Buyer power is about switching and information, not size.** A market of individually tiny
  buyers can still hold enormous power if switching is free and prices are transparent. Look at
  contract length, switching friction and how much the buyer can see before you judge this force.
- **Check who supplies the substitute.** When the industry's own incumbents sell the substitute,
  the threat is internalised: it shifts the revenue mix rather than draining the profit pool. That
  is a different strategic situation from an outsider attacking with a cheaper alternative, and it
  deserves a different rating.
- **Look for the force that is also the rule-maker.** In regulated industries one actor may set
  input prices, control supply of the key input and write the entry conditions. When that is true,
  say so plainly: the supplier force is doing work the other four cannot offset.
- **Recognise the feedback loop.** The chain from structure to performance is not one-way. Strong
  performance funds conduct, such as heavy investment, that can reshape structure in the incumbent's
  favour. Note where a firm is actively bending a force rather than merely living under it.
- **Concentration measures help, but say what they mean.** A share table or a concentration index
  is a fact about the industry; the finding is what the number implies for rivalry and pricing. If
  concentration measured on revenue differs from concentration measured on volume, that difference
  is usually the most informative thing on the page.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating the frameworks as checklists to be filled in rather than forces to be rated and
  explained.
- Drawing the industry boundary after the analysis, so that substitutes and rivals are whatever the
  narrative needs them to be.
- Producing one industry-wide rating when the industry contains segments with genuinely different
  structures.
- Confusing a macro trend with a force. A trend matters here only through the force it moves.
- Assuming high concentration means low rivalry. Concentrated industries can compete ferociously on
  anything except price.
- Calling a force "high" because it sounds dramatic. Rate on the effect on industry profit, not on
  how eventful the force feels.

## Quality checks

- Is the industry boundary stated explicitly, before the force ratings?
- Does every force carry a rating *and* the mechanism that produces it?
- Does at least one macro factor connect to a named force, rather than sitting in its own list?
- If the industry has segments, is the analysis segmented or is the averaging flagged?
- Are the rating drivers specific and checkable, rather than generic industry description?
- Would the analysis change if the reader substituted a different industry? If not, it is generic.

## Standard diagram convention

Draw the five forces as a **cross**, not a list and not five boxes in a row. Competitive rivalry
sits in a large central box. The other four sit north, south, east and west of it, with arrows
pointing **inward** toward the centre. The layout carries meaning: entrants and substitutes arrive
from outside, buyers and suppliers press from the sides, and incumbents fight in the middle.

Colour-code each box by the intensity you assigned, and **print the rating word inside the box as
well**, so the exhibit survives being read in greyscale or by a colour-blind reader. Put the actual
evidence inside each box, two or three lines at most. A box reading "Buyer power: High" is a
template; a box that names the switching-cost and price-transparency figures behind that rating is
an exhibit.

For the macro scan, use a six-cell grid or a radial hexagon around a centre label. Avoid a
hub-and-spoke star, which implies a hierarchy the framework does not have. Leave a cell visibly
empty if you have nothing evidenced for it, rather than filling it to make the picture look
complete.
