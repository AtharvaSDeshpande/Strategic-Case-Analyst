# Adjacencies and new market space

## When to use it

Use this when the question is where growth comes from next. It maps the moves a firm is actually
making, grades each by how much unfamiliar capability it demands, and tests whether a move described
as entering new space really does so.

It is the natural follow-on once the industry looks saturated, because at that point growth has to
come from a new product, a new buyer, or a redrawn boundary.

## The framework steps

### The growth grid

Two axes, products and markets, each either existing or new. Four quadrants, rising in risk:

1. **Market penetration** (existing product, existing market). Lowest risk. More share of the
   current market through price, promotion, distribution or acquisition.
2. **Product development** (new product, existing market). Leverages knowledge of the buyer.
   Requires new capability on the product side.
3. **Market development** (existing product, new market). Leverages the product. Requires new
   capability in reaching and serving an unfamiliar buyer.
4. **Diversification** (new product, new market). Highest risk, because both the offer and the
   buyer are unfamiliar, and the capability must be built or bought.

The grid is a **risk map**, not a menu. Where a firm's moves cluster tells you how much unfamiliar
capability it is taking on. Apply a simple test per move: does the firm still sell to a buyer it
knows, using a capability it has? Two "no" answers is diversification risk however the move is
branded.

### New market space

A **contested** market is one where boundaries are settled and rivals fight over existing demand.
**Uncontested** space is created by moving the boundary so that competition becomes beside the
point. The mechanism is pursuing lower cost and higher buyer value at the same time, which is
possible only by changing what the business does rather than how well it does it.

The working tool is four questions about the industry's standard factors: which to **eliminate**,
which to **reduce** well below standard, which to **raise** well above standard, and which to
**create** that the industry has never offered. Eliminate and reduce fund the move; raise and create
deliver the new value.

## How to apply it well

- **Apply a strict test for uncontested space: the value chain must change.** A new price point, a
  new segment or a new marketing angle is competition inside the existing boundary. Genuine new
  space removes a cost element from the structure *and* creates a value element the industry never
  offered.
- **Use the ownership tell.** If two or more incumbents can enter the supposed new space using
  assets they already own, it was an adjacency, not new space. The firm that got there first has a
  timing and capacity lead inside a contested area, which is a real but different advantage, and
  should be described as such.
- **Separate the category from the firm.** A category can be genuinely new while no individual
  firm's position in it is uncontested. Both readings can be true at once, and conflating them
  overstates the firm's position.
- **Insist on unit economics before calling it value innovation.** Without cost-to-serve, price
  realisation and retention for the new activity, subsidised capacity expansion and genuine value
  innovation look identical from the outside. If those numbers are not disclosed, say the evidence
  cannot settle it rather than choosing the flattering reading.
- **Count the quadrants a firm actually occupies, across rivals too.** The comparison is more
  informative than any single firm's map. A rival present in a quadrant the subject has never
  entered is a finding.
- **Check whether the same asset is being counted twice.** When one investment produces two
  apparent moves, scoring both as independent strengths inflates the assessment.
- **Ask who the buyer unit is.** The same move can be product development or market development
  depending on whether the buyer is the same person in a new guise or a genuinely different buyer.
  State which you have assumed, because it changes the risk grade.
- **Market development often fails on the go-to-market, not the product.** Reaching an unfamiliar
  buyer usually demands a different sales structure, and firms that change the target without
  changing the structure tend to stall.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating the grid as a list of options to pursue rather than a risk map of moves already made.
- Accepting a firm's framing of a move. A move branded as entering a new market is frequently
  penetration of the existing one with new packaging.
- Declaring uncontested space on the strength of a novel product, with no evidence of the cost
  structure changing.
- Assuming adjacency is safe. It is *safer*, and it still requires capability the firm may not have.
- Recommending diversification when the evidence records no diversification move and no capability
  to support one.
- Ignoring that uncontested space becomes contested. The interesting question is usually how long
  the firm has, and what it does with the window.

## Quality checks

- Is every identified move placed in exactly one quadrant, with the buyer unit and the capability
  named?
- Are rivals mapped on the same grid, so the comparison is like-for-like?
- For any claim of uncontested space, is there evidence of both a cost element removed and a value
  element created?
- Is the firm-level claim distinguished from the category-level claim?
- Where unit economics are missing, is that stated as a limit on the conclusion?
- Are quadrants with no evidenced move left empty rather than populated speculatively?

## Standard diagram convention

Draw the growth grid as a **two-by-two**: products on one axis, markets on the other, each split
existing and new. Plot the firm's actual moves as labelled points or small bubbles inside the
quadrants, rather than writing generic quadrant descriptions. A grid that merely names the four
quadrants teaches the framework; a grid carrying this firm's moves analyses it.

Two additions make it useful. Shade the quadrants by risk so the risk gradient is visible. Plot
rivals in a second colour with a clear legend, which immediately exposes quadrants a competitor
occupies and the subject does not.

For the new-market-space analysis, the conventional exhibit is a **value curve**: competing factors
along the horizontal axis, offering level on the vertical, with a line per player. The finding is
the shape of the divergence between the lines, so label the specific factors being eliminated,
reduced, raised and created rather than leaving the curve unannotated. Where a factor's level is not
evidenced, break the line rather than interpolating a plausible point.
