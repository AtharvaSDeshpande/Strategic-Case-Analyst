# Implementation and leadership

## When to use it

Use this when formulation looks sound and results do not follow. Formulation is an analytical task
for a few people; implementation is a coordination task across the whole organisation, and the
skills are different. A strategy that is correct on paper fails routinely because the structure,
systems, people or shared assumptions were never moved to match it.

Also use it when you need to know **whose** position is changing. Implementation risk concentrates
wherever a stakeholder is about to move between quadrants.

## The framework steps

### Alignment across seven elements

Seven interdependent elements. Change one and the others must move with it.

**Hard elements**, relatively visible and directly manageable:

1. **Strategy** — the plan for building and holding advantage.
2. **Structure** — reporting lines, how work is divided and recombined.
3. **Systems** — the processes and routines by which work actually gets done, including how
   performance is measured and capital allocated.

**Soft elements**, cultural and harder to observe:

4. **Shared values** — the assumptions at the centre that the rest are arranged around.
5. **Style** — how leadership actually behaves, as distinct from how it describes itself.
6. **Staff** — who the people are, how they are recruited, developed and rewarded.
7. **Skills** — what the organisation is collectively good at.

The diagnosis is not a description of each element. It is the search for **misalignment**: places
where two elements pull in different directions.

### Operational and leadership requirements

On the operational side, implementation needs long-range goals converted into short-term
objectives, policies that make daily decisions consistent with them, resources genuinely reallocated
rather than nominally assigned, and a structure that matches the strategy.

On the leadership side it needs the ability to secure commitment, manage resistance, and handle
conflict productively rather than suppress it. Analytical strength drives formulation; the ability
to work with people drives implementation.

### Stakeholder grid

Place each stakeholder by **power** to affect the outcome against **interest** in it. Four
quadrants: manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed, monitor.

The grid's real value is dynamic. The useful question is which stakeholder is **about to move**
between quadrants, because relocation is where implementation risk concentrates. Elevating a group's
power deliberately is a legitimate strategic instrument, not merely a description of the status quo.

## How to apply it well

- **Look for the tension, not the inventory.** Seven paragraphs describing seven elements is not a
  diagnosis. Name at least one pair that conflicts and explain the mechanism by which the conflict
  blocks execution.
- **A hard-element change without soft-element support usually stalls.** Mandating a new structure
  or a new standard while the people, incentives and shared assumptions still reward the old way
  produces passive non-compliance rather than open refusal, which is harder to detect and slower to
  surface.
- **Trace a claimed capability to the activity that sustains it.** If an organisation claims a
  distinctive service or culture, find the support activity that produces it: how people are
  recruited, trained, measured and paid. A claimed capability with no traceable support activity is
  an assertion.
- **Check the systems as well as the strategy.** A recurring and easily missed misalignment is that
  the reporting system cannot show whether the new strategy is working, because the new activity is
  reported inside an aggregate. That is a systems-versus-strategy conflict visible only in what the
  disclosure does *not* separate.
- **Name the incoming stakeholder.** Groups that do not exist yet, such as future public
  shareholders after a listing, or a regulator about to acquire a new power, carry no weight today
  and may dominate tomorrow. Their arrival also demotes whoever currently holds unconstrained
  control.
- **Distinguish a funding event from a governance event.** When a firm raises capital it does not
  appear to need, the constraint being relieved is usually accountability rather than money. That
  reading changes what should be recommended.
- **Be honest about what financial disclosure cannot see.** Filings contain no headcount, attrition,
  decision rights or stated values. Three of the seven elements are typically invisible in that
  evidence base. Report them as not evidenced and name the kind of source that would show them.
  Inferring culture from results is the exact error the model exists to prevent.

## Common pitfalls

- Filling in all seven elements regardless of evidence, which converts the missing evidence into
  confident description.
- Treating implementation as a handoff, where a plan is passed to operators without continued
  involvement.
- Assuming that announcing a change produces behaviour change.
- Reading strong results as proof of a strong organisation. Results establish capability; they do
  not establish its source, and they cannot separate organisational excellence from a structural
  advantage that would have produced the same numbers anyway.
- Mapping stakeholders once and treating the grid as static.
- Suppressing conflict rather than using it. Implementation redistributes resources, so friction is
  a signal about where the real constraints are.

## Quality checks

- Is at least one specific misalignment named, with the mechanism explained?
- Is each element marked either evidenced or not evidenced, with the source type named for the gaps?
- Are the soft elements left genuinely empty where the evidence base cannot reach them?
- Does the stakeholder grid identify a position that is changing, not only current positions?
- For each claimed capability, is the sustaining activity identified?
- Is there an explicit statement of what this evidence base can and cannot support?

## Standard diagram convention

For alignment, the conventional exhibit is the **seven-element web**: shared values at the centre,
the other six arranged around it, every element connected to every other. The connecting lines are
the content, so do not draw it as a list or a hierarchy. Where you have identified a specific
misalignment, thicken or colour that particular link and annotate it, so the exhibit carries the
diagnosis rather than the model.

Mark unevidenced elements visibly, with a hatched fill or an explicit "not disclosed", rather than
leaving them looking identical to evidenced ones. That single convention is what stops the diagram
from overstating what is known.

For stakeholders, use a **two-by-two of power against interest** with each stakeholder plotted as a
labelled point. Where a stakeholder's position is expected to move, draw an arrow from the current
to the expected position and label the trigger. The arrows are usually the most informative marks on
the page.
