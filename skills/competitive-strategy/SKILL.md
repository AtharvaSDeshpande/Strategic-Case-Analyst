# Competitive strategy

## When to use it

Use this when the question is *how* a firm competes rather than *where*. It answers three linked
questions: which generic position the firm occupies, which activity in the business actually
produces that position, and whether the position was planned or arrived at.

It pairs naturally with internal assessment. That framework asks whether a resource can sustain an
advantage; this one asks where in the firm's activities the advantage is physically located.

## The framework steps

### Generic position

Two dimensions. The source of advantage is either **cost** or **differentiation**. The scope is
either **broad** or **narrow**. That yields cost leadership, differentiation, and focus in a cost or
a differentiation variant.

- **Cost leadership** means being the lowest-cost producer and winning through scale, process,
  proprietary technology or privileged input access. The firm can then price aggressively for share
  or price at market for margin.
- **Differentiation** means being distinctive on something buyers widely value, and charging a
  premium that more than covers the cost of being distinctive.
- **Focus** means serving a narrow segment and pursuing cost or differentiation inside it.

The classic failure is **stuck in the middle**: pursuing both broad positions at once without
resolving the trade-off, and ending with neither a cost structure nor a premium.

### Value chain

Decompose the firm into activities and find which one carries the advantage.

*Primary activities* run left to right: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics,
marketing and sales, and service. *Support activities* sit across them: procurement, technology
development, human resource management, and firm infrastructure. Margin is what is left when the
buyer values the output above the cost of the activities.

The strongest advantages usually sit in the **linkages** between activities rather than inside any
one of them, because a linkage is harder for a rival to see and harder to copy piecemeal.

### Designed or discovered

A realised position is not proof of an intended one. Two accounts usually fit the same end-state
numbers: a deliberate plan executed as designed, and an emergent pattern discovered through
experiment and feedback that was written up as a plan afterwards. End-state figures evidence the
**position**. They rarely evidence the **intent**.

## How to apply it well

- **Apply a two-limb test before calling anything cost leadership.** First limb: the firm charges
  less than rivals *and* earns an equal or better margin. Lower price on a lower margin is
  discounting, not cost leadership. Second limb: the advantage traces to a named activity that is
  physically done differently, and it widens as volume grows. A firm that passes only the second
  limb has a structural difference but has not yet converted it into a cost position. Say exactly
  that rather than rounding up to "cost leader."
- **Structural redesign beats doing the same thing cheaper.** Real cost advantage usually comes from
  re-engineering an activity out of the chain or replacing it with a different mechanism, not from
  performing the industry's standard activities at a slightly lower cost.
- **Name the linkage, not just the activity.** "Operations is efficient" is description. "This
  choice in operations removes a step in service, and the saving grows with volume" is analysis.
- **A support activity can be the primary source of advantage.** Where the product is really a
  service or an environment, the support activity that produces the workforce or the technology may
  be the strategic driver, with the primary activities merely delivering it. Do not assume the
  advantage lives among the primary activities.
- **Vertical integration can be a response to missing markets.** Where reliable external suppliers,
  logistics or intermediaries do not exist, owning more of the chain is a rational answer to the
  environment rather than empire-building. Judge integration against what the external market can
  actually supply.
- **A narrow focus can be a route to broad scale.** Extreme standardisation inside a narrow segment
  is often how a firm builds the volume that later supports a broad cost position. Treat focus as a
  possible stage, not only as a permanent choice.
- **Hold the two readings of the outcome side by side.** State the deliberate account and the
  emergent account, then say which the evidence can actually settle and which it cannot. This
  matters because it changes the recommendation: if the position was designed, the gap is
  execution; if it was discovered, the question is whether the firm can discover the next one.
- **Mark what the chain cannot see.** Procurement terms, input costs and service quality are often
  undisclosed. An honest value chain shows those activities as empty rather than filling them with
  plausible description.

## Common pitfalls

- Calling the largest firm the cost leader. Size is not a cost position; margin evidence is.
- Optimising activities one at a time and never examining how they interact.
- Filling every box of the value chain, which produces a diagram that would look identical for any
  firm in the industry.
- Reading a firm's own strategy statement as evidence of the position it actually occupies.
- Attributing a realised position to a master plan because the design account is the more available
  story and the only one disclosures tend to support.
- Declaring "stuck in the middle" whenever a firm does two things. The diagnosis requires evidence
  of the trade-off being unresolved: mid price *and* mid margin.

## Quality checks

- Is the generic position stated as a single verdict, with the two-limb test shown?
- Does the value chain identify at least one activity that is genuinely done differently, with the
  mechanism explained?
- Are undisclosed activities marked as undisclosed rather than described generically?
- Is at least one linkage between activities named?
- Where intent is claimed, is there evidence of intent, or only of outcome?
- Could the analysis be lifted onto a competitor unchanged? If so, it describes the industry rather
  than the firm.

## Standard diagram convention

Draw the value chain in the conventional shape: **support activities as full-width horizontal
bands** across the top, **primary activities as left-to-right chevrons** beneath them, and a
**margin arrowhead** on the right edge. Do not substitute a stack of plain boxes; the chevron shape
carries the flow.

Two conventions make it analytical rather than decorative. First, **encode evidence in the fill**:
solid fill where a cited figure supports the content, and a hatched or greyed fill where the
activity is not disclosed. An exhibit that silently fills every box overstates what is known.
Second, **print the number inside the activity**, so the diagram carries findings rather than
labels. If the analysis compares margins, add a small margin strip on the right so the comparison
that drives the verdict is visible on the same page.

For the generic position itself, a two-by-two of advantage source against competitive scope is
sufficient, with the firm and its named rivals plotted as points. Plot rivals too: a single point
on a positioning grid asserts a position, whereas several points demonstrate one.
