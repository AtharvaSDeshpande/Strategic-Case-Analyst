# Diversification and corporate strategy

## When to use it

Competitive strategy asks how to win in one market. Corporate strategy asks a different question:
which businesses should the parent own, and what does the parent add that the businesses would not
have standing alone. Use this for portfolio composition, capital allocation between units, and any
assessment of a diversified group.

The institutional part applies wherever the surrounding market infrastructure is uneven, and it
explains structures that look irrational when judged only against a mature-market benchmark.

## The framework steps

### Related or unrelated

**Related diversification** expands into businesses whose activities share something strategically
meaningful with the existing ones. Value comes from two mechanisms:

- **Economies of scope**: sharing a capability, facility or network makes running both businesses
  together cheaper than running them apart.
- **Cross-selling**: selling several units' offerings to the same customer raises revenue per
  customer and can deepen lock-in.

**Unrelated diversification** expands into businesses with no such fit. The logic is financial:
spreading risk, and allocating capital internally.

Both mechanisms are **cost and revenue facts**. Neither is a branding fact. A shared name over
separately run units is unrelated diversification wearing related diversification's clothes.

### Portfolio matrix

Plot each business on market growth (a proxy for attractiveness) against relative market share
measured against the largest competitor (a proxy for competitive strength). Four quadrants:

- **Question marks**: high growth, low share. Cash absorbing. Invest to build share, or exit.
- **Stars**: high growth, high share. Generate cash and consume it defending position.
- **Cash cows**: low growth, high share. Fund the rest.
- **Dogs**: low growth, low share. Release the capital.

The portfolio only works as a **pipeline**: cows fund question marks, which become stars, which
mature into the next generation of cows. A portfolio read as a static snapshot misses the point.

### Institutional environment

Strategy is shaped by the rules of the game: laws and regulation, social norms, and shared
expectations. Where external institutions such as deep capital markets, reliable contract
enforcement, skilled-labour pools or specialist intermediaries are underdeveloped, firms face
**institutional voids** and substitute internally, building their own capital allocation, talent
pipelines and supply chains.

This drives a reversal worth knowing. Where voids are wide, diversified groups can trade at a
**premium**, because the group structure supplies what the market does not. As institutions mature,
external alternatives outperform the internal substitutes, the costs of managing a sprawling
portfolio start to bite, and the premium turns into a **discount**.

## How to apply it well

- **Admit an ecosystem claim only with a shared mechanism or a shared line item.** Common ownership
  and common reporting are evidence of structure. Cross-sell and lock-in require evidence that the
  same customer buys across units, or that a shared resource lowers cost. Audience size, brand
  reach and a co-branded app are not scope economies.
- **Test whether the parent is willing, not merely present.** Group affiliation on its own buys
  nothing. What shows up in the numbers is a parent that concentrates capital in one business.
  Affiliated firms starved of capital are the control case that disproves the naive version.
- **Separate capacity to invest from return on investment.** Falling leverage alongside falling
  return on capital is a real pattern and it means the firm is getting safer and less productive at
  once. Report both; either alone misleads.
- **Do not claim a cross-subsidy you cannot trace.** Group-level capital expenditure is not evidence
  of a transfer from one unit to another. If the disclosure is group-level only, say so.
- **Check before importing void logic.** A functioning regulator publishing unit-level data, a
  working route to public capital, and institutional investors buying in directly are the *opposite*
  of voids. Institutional theory over-predicts if applied to an environment whose relevant voids
  have already closed. Ask which voids are actually open now.
- **Watch for the political variable.** Where an obligation of unusual scale sits outside the
  banking system, the resolution may be an administrative decision rather than a market one. That
  makes a rival's survival partly a policy question, which no industry-structure framework captures.
- **Beware milking a star.** Extracting cash from a high-growth, high-share business to fund
  elsewhere is the standard way to turn tomorrow's cash cow into a dog.
- **Relative share means relative to the largest rival.** Absolute share plotted on that axis
  quietly changes what the matrix says.

## Common pitfalls

- Assuming surface similarity produces synergy. Without active management forcing shared resources,
  related diversification decays into unrelated diversification carrying extra overhead.
- Reading the portfolio as a static picture instead of a funding pipeline.
- Using the matrix for rivals in a single industry. It is built for units facing *different*
  industries; with one industry the attractiveness axis is constant and the exhibit collapses.
- Treating a conglomerate as automatically inefficient. That judgment imports a mature-market
  benchmark into an environment where it may not apply.
- Explaining a firm's position by its parent when no transfer is evidenced.
- Plotting bubbles whose position or size comes from estimate rather than disclosure.

## Quality checks

- Is each unit's quadrant driven by two stated, sourced numbers?
- Is the pipeline argument explicit: which unit funds which?
- For every claimed scope economy or cross-sell, is there a shared cost line or a shared customer?
- If institutional voids are invoked, is each claimed void shown to be open *now*?
- Are capacity-to-invest and return-on-capital reported together?
- Where the matrix does not fit the question, is a different instrument chosen and the reason given?

## Standard diagram convention

The portfolio matrix is a **two-by-two bubble chart**. Relative market share on the horizontal axis,
conventionally descending left to right so that strength sits on the left; market growth on the
vertical. **Bubble area encodes a third variable**, normally revenue or capital employed, and the
convention only works if you state which. Label the quadrants, and use arrows to show expected
movement over time where you have grounds for it.

Every bubble's position and size must come from a disclosed figure. If an axis value is not
available, either leave that axis unquantified and say so in the caption, or leave the unit off. An
estimated bubble is indistinguishable from a measured one once drawn, which makes this the exhibit
where invented numbers do the most damage.

Where the comparison is several rivals inside **one** industry, do not use a nine-box or a
growth-share matrix. Use a weighted competitive profile matrix instead: criteria as rows, weights
summing to one, a score per firm, and a weighted total. Print the criteria and the weights on the
exhibit, because a score whose weighting is hidden is an assertion.
