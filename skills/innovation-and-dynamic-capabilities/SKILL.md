# Innovation and dynamic capabilities

## When to use it

Static frameworks tell you whether a firm has an advantage now. Use this one when the environment is
moving and the question is whether the firm can keep earning that advantage. It adds time to the
analysis: it asks whether a position was bought with capital whose return is falling, and whether
the next reconfiguration has been executed or only filed.

It is the natural companion to internal assessment. That framework asks whether the organisation can
capture value from a resource today; this one explains how a firm keeps repairing that fit as the
environment shifts.

## The framework steps

Three clusters, in sequence.

1. **Sensing.** The capacity to scan markets and technologies and identify a shift before it is
   obvious. Evidence looks like early positions in categories others have not entered, research
   output, or a move timed ahead of the field.
2. **Seizing.** The capacity to commit capital, redesign the business model and mobilise the
   organisation to capture what was sensed. Evidence looks like capital actually deployed, capacity
   actually built, customers actually moved.
3. **Transforming.** The continuous capacity to reconfigure assets, structures and routines so the
   firm is not trapped by its own history. Evidence looks like a restructure completed, a unit
   divested, a revenue architecture changed.

Alongside runs **ambidexterity**: running **exploitation** of the existing business and
**exploration** of the next one at the same time, which is difficult because the two require
different metrics, time horizons and tolerances for failure.

The useful relationship to internal assessment: dynamic capabilities are the mechanism that keeps
the organisational fit current. A valuable, rare and inimitable resource decays into a non-advantage
when the organisation around it stops matching the market, and the transforming cluster is what
repairs it.

## How to apply it well

- **Rate the evidence for each cluster separately: strong, partial or absent.** Sensing and seizing
  are cheap to claim and comparatively easy to evidence. Transforming is the one firms announce and
  do not finish. Grading them together hides exactly the gap worth reporting.
- **Distinguish financial reconfiguration from organisational reconfiguration.** A rebuilt balance
  sheet is genuine transforming, and it is not the same as a changed operating model or revenue
  architecture. If only the financial kind is evidenced, say that plainly rather than claiming the
  capability in general.
- **A filing is not a completed reconfiguration.** Announced, filed and approved are three different
  states. Report which one the evidence supports as at the date of the source.
- **Do not claim ambidexterity without exploration outcomes.** Audience, user counts or reach for a
  new venture are activity measures. Without revenue, margin or retention attributable to it, the
  capability can be neither claimed nor denied, and saying so is the correct finding.
- **Use a control case.** A rival that senses correctly but cannot seize, because its balance sheet
  will not allow it, demonstrates that seizing capacity is largely a funding question. That
  comparison usually teaches more than either firm examined alone.
- **Look for the competency trap early.** The signature is a firm continuing to excel at the thing
  that is becoming worth less per unit: volume still growing, return on capital drifting down,
  value migrating to a tier where a rival is stronger. Falling returns on capital alongside
  continued operational success is the early warning, and it is easy to mistake for success.
- **Separate mistiming from incapacity.** A heavy investment phase produces falling returns for
  ordinary reasons. Before diagnosing a capability gap, ask whether the pattern is simply an asset
  base not yet at steady-state use, and state honestly whether the available observations can settle
  it. Three annual data points usually cannot.
- **Sensing without seizing is a specific, common failure.** A firm can identify a shift correctly
  and still fail to move capital away from a profitable legacy business. When you see it, locate the
  blockage: capital allocation, structure, or leadership willingness.

## Common pitfalls

- Reading a narrative of adaptation as evidence of capability. The framework is a test, not a story
  template.
- Claiming transforming on the basis of intent, strategy documents or announcements.
- Treating any acquisition as seizing. Seizing requires the business model to change, not just the
  asset register.
- Assuming exploitation and exploration can share metrics and governance.
- Using the framework only where it flatters. A firm that senses well and transforms badly is the
  normal case, and reporting that is the contribution.
- Confusing operational capability, doing current things well, with dynamic capability, changing
  what the firm does.

## Quality checks

- Does each of the three clusters carry an explicit rating and the evidence behind it?
- Is any cluster rated on announcement rather than execution?
- Is the exploitation/exploration comparison supported by outcome evidence on both sides, or is the
  imbalance stated?
- Is the competency-trap reading marked as inference rather than reported as fact?
- Is the strongest alternative explanation, that this is an investment cycle ending, stated and
  tested?
- Would the same evidence support the opposite conclusion? If so, report the ambiguity.

## Standard diagram convention

The conventional exhibit is a **left-to-right three-stage flow**: sensing, then seizing, then
transforming, with arrows between them and a feedback arrow returning from transforming to sensing,
since the cycle repeats. Do not draw it as a triangle or a pyramid; the sequence and the loop are
the content.

Make it analytical by **colour-coding each stage by evidence strength**, with the rating word
printed inside as well as encoded in colour, and by listing the specific evidence under each stage
rather than the textbook definition. A stage rated absent should look visibly different, not merely
carry less text.

For ambidexterity, a two-column comparison of exploitation against exploration works better than a
diagram, with the same metric requested of both columns. Where the exploration column has only
activity measures and no outcome measures, leave the outcome row visibly empty. That empty row is
usually the finding.
