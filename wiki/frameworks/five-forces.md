# Five Forces

Accumulated guidance on applying the industry-structure analysis well. Definitions belong in the
per-case concept note; this page holds method.

## How to apply this well

- Define the industry boundary explicitly **before** rating anything. Substitutes and rivals are
  determined by that boundary, so drawing it afterwards lets the analysis shape its own answer.
- Never present it as a static snapshot. Rate the direction of travel as well as the level, and
  note where a firm is actively bending a force rather than living under it (for example by
  integrating backwards to reduce supplier power).
- Buyer power is driven by switching costs and information, not by buyer size. Many small,
  well-informed buyers with nothing holding them in place can hold more power than a few large ones
  on long contracts.
- Check who supplies the substitute. When incumbents sell it themselves, the threat is internalised
  and shifts the revenue mix rather than draining the profit pool. That deserves a different rating
  and a different recommendation.
- Rate on the effect on industry profit, not on how dramatic the force feels.
- One rating per force describes no individual firm in an industry with wide performance dispersion.
  Say so explicitly rather than letting the average stand.
- Concentration statistics are inputs, not findings. Where concentration measured on revenue differs
  from concentration measured on volume, that difference is usually the most informative result.

## Signals that the application is weak

- Five ratings with no mechanism attached to any of them.
- An industry boundary that is never stated.
- A rating that would not change if the reader substituted a different industry.


## 2026-09-24
- **Application Learning**: The Threat of Substitutes can be neutralized not just by building a better product, but by altering the buyer's economics. Bundling a substitute for free to an existing massive user base effectively destroys the substitute's go-to-market motion.


## 2026-09-24 - Software Lock-in as a Moat
- Taught that buyer power can be neutralized not just by lack of alternatives, but by the prohibitive switching costs of retraining developers and rewriting legacy code on a proprietary platform.
